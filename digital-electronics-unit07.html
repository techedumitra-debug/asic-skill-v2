Detailed study · Combinational Logic Design

Combinational logic computes its outputs from the current input vector. A truth table specifies behavior independently of the gates chosen, while a Boolean equation describes one implementation. Physical gate outputs settle only after finite propagation time.

Step-by-step design method

  1. List every input and output, including enable and valid signals
  2. Enumerate all 2ⁿ input combinations or specify the legal subset
  3. Derive an expression for each output and minimize it
  4. Draw connected gates or synthesize RTL
  5. Simulate every legal input, then assess delay, fanout, and hazards.

Worked example

A three-input majority detector outputs 1 when at least two inputs are 1. For A,B,C = 1,0,1, the result is 1 because two inputs are asserted.

Result: Y = AB + AC + BC. Its on-set is m(3,5,6,7), using A as the most significant input bit.

Engineering consideration: A simplified expression is functionally equivalent only after all required combinations are checked. Do not assume a short expression guarantees minimum propagation delay.
Check your understanding: For the majority function, what is Y when A,B,C = 0,1,0?

Y=0 because only one of the three inputs is high.

Detailed study · Combinational versus sequential logic

A purely combinational block has no stored state. Its output is a function F(X) of present inputs X; a sequential machine has state Q and uses next-state logic Q⁺=G(Q,X).

Step-by-step design method

NAND and NOR are functionally complete: either gate family can realize NOT, AND, OR, and XOR. The half-adder therefore needs no dedicated XOR cell if only universal gates are available.

Detailed study · Full-adder: carry-in and carry-out

The full-adder sums three one-bit operands A, B, and carry-in Cin. Sum indicates odd parity; carry-out Cout is high when two or more of the inputs are high.

Step-by-step design method

  1. Compute P=A⊕B
  2. Then S=P⊕Cin
  3. Compute G=A·B

  5. Test all eight input combinations.

For A=1,B=1,Cin=1, 1+1+1=3=11₂.

Result: S=1 and Cout=1. For A=1,B=0,Cin=1, the result is 10₂.

Four-bit parallel adder

Multiple full-adder stages process corresponding A and B bits at once. Carry still flows between stages unless additional look-ahead logic is present.

Four-bit parallel adder · live circuitCHANGE INPUTS
Design relationship

The four-bit sum ranges from 0 to 31 with carry-in, so preserve Cout in a fifth result bit.

Detailed study · Four-bit parallel adder

An n-bit parallel adder places n one-bit full-adders side by side so the operand bits arrive together. In a basic ripple-carry realization, each stage still depends on carry from its less significant neighbor.

Step-by-step design method

  1. Align A₃…A₀ and B₃…B₀ by bit position
  2. Set initial C₀
  3. Calculate each Sᵢ=Aᵢ⊕Bᵢ⊕Cᵢ and Cᵢ₊₁=AᵢBᵢ+Cᵢ(Aᵢ⊕Bᵢ)
  4. Keep the final carry.

Worked example

A=1011₂ (11), B=0110₂ (6), Cin=0 gives 10001₂ (17).

Result: Four-bit sum S=0001; final Cout=1. The five-bit total is {Cout,S}=10001.

Engineering consideration: For unsigned 4-bit operands, sum can require five bits; dropping Cout would make 11+6 appear as 1.
Check your understanding: Add A=0101 and B=0011, Cin=0.

S=1000, Cout=0 (decimal 8).

RELATED TOPIC · RIPPLE CARRY

Ripple-carry timing and stage propagation

Each carry-out becomes the next stage's carry-in. Later sum bits may remain unsettled until carry has passed through preceding stages.

Ripple-carry timing and stage propagation · live circuitCHANGE INPUTS
Design relationship

For an n-stage chain, the critical carry path can cross n full-adder carry arcs.

Detailed study · Ripple-carry timing and stage propagation

In ripple carry, carry is generated in the least significant stage and may travel through each more significant stage before the top sum settles. The worst-case carry chain limits speed.

Step-by-step design method

  1. Determine each stage’s propagate Pᵢ=Aᵢ⊕Bᵢ and generate Gᵢ=AᵢBᵢ
  2. Propagate an input carry through any Pᵢ=1 stage
  3. Identify the longest chain and compare with timing requirements.

Worked example

For A=1111 and B=0001, a carry generated in bit 0 must traverse bits 1, 2, and 3.

Result: The result is 1_0000₂; the final carry reaches Cout after the chain delay.

Engineering consideration: Individual sum bits may momentarily show intermediate values while carry is rippling. Use the settled result for the arithmetic truth table.
Check your understanding: Which input pattern can force carry through four 4-bit stages?

A=1111, B=0001, Cin=0 is a classic example.

A programmable adder/subtractor uses control M to conditionally invert B and to supply the initial carry. Thus a shared datapath performs two arithmetic functions.

Detailed study · Carry-lookahead adder: generate and propagate

Carry-lookahead logic computes carries directly from group generate and propagate terms. It reduces serial carry dependency at the cost of additional combinational fan-in and wiring.

Step-by-step design method

  1. Define Gᵢ=AᵢBᵢ and Pᵢ=Aᵢ⊕Bᵢ
  2. Expand C₁=G₀+P₀C₀, C₂=G₁+P₁G₀+P₁P₀C₀
  3. Continue the expansion for higher stages.

Worked example

Take A₁A₀=11, B₁B₀=01 and C₀=0. At bit 0 G₀=1, so C₁=1; bit 1 P₁=1, so C₂=1.

Result: 11₂ + 01₂ = 100₂; S₁S₀=00 and C₂=1.

Engineering consideration: Carry-lookahead improves theoretical depth but a physical implementation must account for large AND/OR networks and interconnect delay.
Check your understanding: Write the carry-out equation for a single stage.

Detailed study · Serial addition: one bit per clock

A serial adder processes one bit per clock interval, usually least significant first. Two shift registers supply operand bits and one carry flip-flop retains the carry for the next time step.

Step-by-step design method

  1. Initialize carry register to 0
  2. Shift Aᵢ and Bᵢ into a full-adder
  3. Write sum bit
  4. Latch carry-out
  5. Repeat for n clock cycles and include extra carry if required.

A=1011 and B=0011: from LSB to MSB, pairs (1,1),(1,1),(0,0),(1,0) produce sums 0,1,1,1 and final carry 0.

RELATED TOPIC · SERIAL PARALLEL

Detailed study · Serial and parallel adder comparison

Parallel adders operate on all bit pairs in one arithmetic operation, whereas serial adders reuse a small amount of hardware across multiple cycles. Their area, latency and throughput differ.

Step-by-step design method

  1. Compare number of full-adder cells, register requirements and carry mechanism
  2. Estimate latency in cycles
  3. Evaluate target clock frequency, power and input/output serialization.

Worked example

An eight-bit parallel ripple adder uses eight full-adder stages and completes one addition combinationally; a serial adder can reuse one full-adder over eight bit cycles.

Result: The serial approach saves arithmetic logic but needs shift registers, carry storage and scheduling.

Engineering consideration: Do not claim that serial always saves total area: storage and control overhead can dominate at very small word widths.
Check your understanding: Which style is attractive when minimizing latency for an eight-bit sum?

A parallel adder, subject to its carry-path delay.
